# A real quadratic system as a single quartic

## Problem

Fix $n$ variables and finitely many polynomials $p_0, \dots, p_{m-1}$ in
$\mathbb{R}[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, each of total degree at most $2$. One polynomial
of total degree at most $4$ cuts out the same real zero set:

$$\exists f \in \mathbb{R}[x_1, \dots, x_n], \quad \deg f \le 4
\ \text{ and } \ \forall x \in \mathbb{R}^n,
\ \bigl( f(x) = 0 \iff \forall i,\ p_i(x) = 0 \bigr).$$

## The idea

Add up the squares:

$$f = \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} p_i^2.$$

A finite sum of real squares vanishes exactly when every summand vanishes, so
$f$ computes the conjunction of the $m$ equations. Squaring doubles the degree,
which takes $2$ to $4$.

## Where it comes from

The problem asks for one equation equivalent to a conjunction of $m$ equations.
So the real question is which algebraic operation on polynomials computes an
intersection of zero sets.

Multiplication is the first thing to try, and it computes the wrong set. The
product $p_0 p_1$ vanishes on the union of the two zero sets, because a field
has no zero divisors. Addition on its own is worse, since $p_0 + p_1$ vanishes
wherever the two cancel.

What is left is the order on $\mathbb{R}$, and the order supplies exactly one
useful operation. A square is nonnegative, and it is zero only at zero. A sum
of several squares can therefore vanish only when every term does, because no
term can cancel another. That is a conjunction, written
algebraically, and the ordering of $\mathbb{R}$ is the entire reason it works.

Now count the degree. The sum of squares is the cheapest AND gate the ordering
offers, and its price is a factor of two in degree. Given quadratics it
returns a quartic. Degree $4$ is not an artifact of the proof, then. It is what
the cheapest available construction costs, starting from degree $2$.

That price is the point of the statement. In the Blum, Shub, and Smale model
of computation over $\mathbb{R}$, the standard complete problem for the class
$\text{NP}_{\mathbb{R}}$ is the feasibility of a single real polynomial of
degree $4$. A reduction that lands on degree $4$ therefore lands exactly on it.
A construction costing a factor of three in degree would produce sextics and
prove nothing about that class.

## The proof

The construction splits into a bound and an equivalence, and neither half needs
the other.

**The degree bound.** Total degree is subadditive under multiplication, so
$\deg(p_i^2) \le 2 \deg p_i \le 4$ for every $i$. The total degree of a finite
sum is at most the largest total degree of a summand, so $\deg f \le 4$. Both
inequalities are non-strict, and neither asks any $p_i$ to be nonzero.

**The equivalence.** Fix $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Evaluation at $x$ is a ring
homomorphism, so

$$f(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} p_i(x)^2 .$$

Every summand is a real square, hence nonnegative, so the sum vanishes only
when every summand vanishes. In $\mathbb{R}$ a square vanishes only when its
base does. Therefore

$$f(x) = 0 \iff \forall i, p_i(x)^2 = 0 \iff \forall i, p_i(x) = 0 .$$

This is an equivalence at each point, so it gives equality of zero sets and not
merely one inclusion.

## Why the hypotheses bite

**The degree hypothesis feeds the bound alone.** The equivalence half never
reads $\deg p_i \le 2$. The sum of squares cuts out the same zero set for any
finite family of real polynomials of any degrees. Without the hypothesis the theorem still
holds, with $4$ replaced by twice the largest degree.

**Finiteness feeds the witness, not the theorem.** An infinite family has no
sum of squares inside the polynomial ring, so this witness needs $m$ finite.
The existence claim survives anyway, and here is the cheap corollary.
$\mathbb{R}[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is Noetherian, so the ideal generated by an
infinite family $\{p_i\}$ is generated by finitely many $q_1, \dots, q_k$. A
point kills every $p_i$ exactly when it kills every element of that ideal, so
$\sum_j q_j^2$ cuts out the same real zero set. What is lost is the bound:
nothing controls the degrees of the $q_j$.

**The ordering is essential, and $\mathbb{C}$ is the case that shows it.** The
usual thing to say here is that the sum of squares fails over $\mathbb{C}$: for
$p_0 = x_1$ and $p_1 = i x_1$ the sum $p_0^2 + p_1^2$ is identically zero,
while the system cuts out the origin. That only retires one witness. The
stronger statement holds, and it is short.

**Claim.** Over $\mathbb{C}$ the theorem is false. No polynomial
$f \in \mathbb{C}[x_1, x_2]$ at all, of any degree, has zero set exactly
$\{(0,0)\}$, while $p_0 = x_1$ and $p_1 = x_2$ cut that point out.

*Proof.* Write $f = \sum_{j=0}^{d} c_j(x_1) x_2^j$ with $c_d \ne 0$ as a
polynomial in $x_2$ over $\mathbb{C}[x_1]$.

Suppose $d \ge 1$. The polynomial $c_d$ is nonzero, so it has finitely many
roots, and for every other value $a$ the one-variable polynomial $f(a, x_2)$
has degree exactly $d \ge 1$. Since $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed it has
a root $b$, and $(a,b)$ lies in the zero set. Distinct values of $a$ give
distinct points, so the zero set is infinite.

Suppose instead $d = 0$, so $f = c_0(x_1)$ depends on $x_1$ alone. If $c_0$ is
a nonzero constant the zero set is empty. If $c_0$ is the zero polynomial the
zero set is all of $\mathbb{C}^2$. Otherwise the zero set is a finite nonempty
union of vertical lines, which is infinite.

In every case the zero set is empty, infinite, or everything, so it is never
the single point $\{(0,0)\}$. $\square$

So the pivot is not a shortage of witnesses over $\mathbb{C}$. An ordered field
lets a finite conjunction of equations collapse to one equation, and an
algebraically closed field forbids it, because a hypersurface there is either
empty or of dimension $n-1$. The construction belongs to real algebraic
geometry.

## Formalization

The Lean is routine, which is the honest report. `MvPolynomial.totalDegree_pow`
and `MvPolynomial.totalDegree_finsetSum` give the two halves of the degree
bound directly. `Finset.sum_eq_zero_iff_of_nonneg`, fed `sq_nonneg` at each
index, is the ordering argument already packaged in Mathlib, and it is the only
step where the real numbers do any work.

```lean
theorem solution {n m : ℕ} (p : Fin m → MvPolynomial (Fin n) ℝ)
    (hdeg : ∀ i, (p i).totalDegree ≤ 2) :
    ∃ f : MvPolynomial (Fin n) ℝ, f.totalDegree ≤ 4 ∧
      ∀ x : Fin n → ℝ,
        (MvPolynomial.eval x f = 0 ↔ ∀ i, MvPolynomial.eval x (p i) = 0) := by
  refine ⟨∑ i, p i ^ 2, ?_, ?_⟩
  · refine le_trans (MvPolynomial.totalDegree_finsetSum _ _) (Finset.sup_le fun i _ => ?_)
    calc (p i ^ 2).totalDegree
        ≤ 2 * (p i).totalDegree := MvPolynomial.totalDegree_pow _ _
      _ ≤ 2 * 2 := by gcongr; exact hdeg i
      _ = 4 := by norm_num
  · intro x
    rw [map_sum]
    simp only [map_pow]
    rw [Finset.sum_eq_zero_iff_of_nonneg fun i _ => sq_nonneg (eval x (p i))]
    simp
```

One transferable detail. The statement is restated in the solution file instead
of imported from the upstream statement file. That file proves its theorem with
`sorry`, and importing it would pull `sorryAx` into the axiom closure of
everything downstream, so the audit would pass while proving nothing.

## What it does not say

The theorem is an existence claim, and the sum of squares is one witness for
it. Nothing above claims $4$ is optimal for a given system, and nothing claims
$f$ is unique.

The degree-$4$ part of $f$ is the sum of the squares of the degree-$2$ leading
forms of the $p_i$. A sum of squares of real forms vanishes only when every
form does, so the bound is attained as soon as some $p_i$ has total degree
exactly $2$.

An existence statement about zero sets is the kind of claim that can hold
vacuously, so the solution file carries two companion examples at concrete
data. The first instantiates the system $x_1 = 0$, $1 = 0$, which has no real
solution, and concludes that the quartic vanishes nowhere. The second
instantiates $x_1 = 0$, which has the single solution $0$, and concludes that
the quartic vanishes at $0$ and not at $1$. Together they exercise both
directions of the equivalence at named real points, which no vacuous statement
could do.

Two degenerate cases are covered by the same witness with no case split. For
$m = 0$ the system imposes no condition, the right side of the equivalence is
vacuously true, and $f = 0$ vanishes everywhere. For $n = 0$ the space
$\mathbb{R}^0$ is a single point and the equivalence is one scalar identity.

The proof uses no `decide`, no `native_decide`, and no hand-written `axiom`.
The axiom closure stays within `propext`, `Classical.choice`, and `Quot.sound`.
